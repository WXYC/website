WXYC for Credit

How to turn work at the radio station into an independent study, a practicum, a field experience, or a funded summer of research at UNC.

For UNC–Chapel Hill undergraduates · Course details from the 2026–27 catalog, checked August 2026 · Next realistic term: Spring 2027

The station's software is built and maintained by DJs, and there is more to do than there are people to do it. This guide is about how to get academic credit for taking on some of it — and what's available to take on.

There are about ten routes to that credit — client courses where you build something the station will actually use, independent studies, field experiences, even a funded summer of research — and every one of them is open to a student who plans one term ahead. Start with the projects in §1 and find the one you'd want to spend a semester on. Then §2 matches you to a route, and the rest of the guide walks you through the paperwork in order.

Nearly every route requires a signed learning contract finalized in the first week of classes, and the research route (URES 295) requires it two to four weeks before classes begin. Plan one term ahead.

1The project menu

Sixteen semester-sized projects drawn from the station's repositories and planning documents, grouped by what you would be practicing. Every one maps to a written plan, an existing proposal, or a scoping conversation the station can have on demand — and several have complete specs waiting for someone to execute them. This is a sample, not the live list: ask the station what's open, because priorities move.

self-contained means no production access and low blast radius — the safe default for a first-time student. supervised means real data, credentials, PII, or licensing questions are in play — you would work against a fixture or a copy wherever one exists. The Fits column names the credit routes each project suits; every course code links to its details later in the guide.

1.5 The four flowsheet projects are one pipeline, not four versions of the same idea

Four projects above touch the same corpus — 1990–2001 DJ logs, handwritten, run through a vision model. At a glance they look like overlapping takes on "digitize the flowsheets." They are not. Each answers a different question, and they answer them in order:

  1. The blind multi-reviewer verifier asks: how good is the ground truth? When a single volunteer transcribes a page, their reading simply becomes the truth, and you learn nothing about how reliable that is. Having two or three people read the same page independently, and measuring where they disagree, produces both a better transcript and a number — the human noise floor.
  2. The calibration study asks: how good is the machine? It scores model variants against those hand-transcribed pages. This is exactly why it depends on the stage above: "the model disagrees with truth on 8% of rows" is uninterpretable until you know that two careful humans disagree with each other on 5% of rows. Without the noise floor you are measuring the model against an assumption of perfect truth, and your answer is wrong by an unknown amount.
  3. Entity resolution asks something different: what is this record? The first two are about transcription — did we read the handwriting correctly? This one begins after the reading is already correct and asks which row in a fifty-year catalog the words "Guided by Voices — Alien Lanes" actually refer to, given rotation codes, abbreviations, and multiple pressings of the same album. That is record linkage, not handwriting recognition: different methods, different literature, different failure modes.
  4. The historical analysis asks: so what? It touches no OCR at all. It consumes the finished corpus as research data and asks how a freeform station's taste moved across three decades and several DJ generations.

They are stages, then, not duplicates — and that has three consequences for picking one:

  • They are sized and shaped differently. Stage 1 is a build-and-operate project with real people in it. Stage 2 is a contained experiment and the cleanest single-semester deliverable. Stage 3 is the most intellectually open — what an automatic correction should be permitted to do is genuinely unsettled, which is why the first attempt was parked. Stage 4 is a paper.
  • Later stages depend on earlier ones being done well, but you don't have to wait your turn. Stages 1 and 2 can run concurrently by two students who talk to each other — arguably the ideal pairing, and a good argument for the shared-project route in §11, where one faculty sponsor covers you both.
  • Only stages 2 and 3 are single-person, single-semester projects with no dependency on recruiting anyone. If you want the cleanest possible start, take one of those.

★ = the strongest openers for a first-time student. Three projects have a complete written specification already waiting for an executor rather than a scoping conversation: the OCR verifier, the archive-reconciler package, and the fixity tooling.

Pick something finite. The projects that finish are the ones with a clear "done": a screen that works, a report that exists, a number that got better. The ones that stall are open-ended platform rewrites. Your sponsor is evaluating one semester, not a roadmap.

2Start here: which route is yours

Read the five sentences below and take the first one that describes you.

  • "I want to try this without committing to much, and I'd rather not go hunting for a professor." → URES 295. One credit, pass/fail, repeatable twice, satisfies the RESEARCH gen-ed, and it is the only route on this page where a non-faculty WXYC mentor can sign as your mentor of record. If you are unsure, this is the door.
  • "I'm a CS major and I want this to count." → COMP 393 to build something for the station as a real client; COMP 523 to join a senior team and have WXYC pitch you a project; COMP 495 if the work has a research question in it — that's the only CS independent study that counts toward the major, and only once.
  • "I care about the library, the archive, the metadata, or the taxonomy." → INLS 396 for a graded independent study with no prerequisites, or INLS 393 for a 135-hour field experience if you're a junior with the prereqs. Pair either with INLS 651.
  • "I want to design something and test it with real users." → INLS 396 with a UX brief, or take one of the UX courses in SDIS or Hussman and bring a WXYC surface as your project.
  • "I want money and a summer." → SURF. Roughly $4,000 for at least nine weeks, applications due in early February, and it requires a UNC faculty research advisor — so that conversation happens over winter break at the latest.

These are not mutually exclusive across terms. A common arc is URES 295 in one semester to prove the collaboration works, then INLS 396 or COMP 495 for a larger graded project, then an honors thesis built on top of both.

3The three things that stop people

Almost nobody has done this, and the reasons are structural rather than personal. Knowing them is most of the battle:

  1. The window closes before curiosity opens. Every for-credit path at UNC needs a signed learning contract finalized by roughly the first week of classes — and URES needs it weeks before classes start. If you get interested in week four, you have already missed the semester. The fix is to treat this as a decision you make one term early.
  2. You have to find a faculty sponsor yourself. Almost every route needs a UNC faculty instructor of record, and departments do no matchmaking. "Go convince a professor" is where most independent studies quietly die. See §11 — and note that faculty are capped at two independent-study projects per semester, but a single project can hold several students, so "I'm full" is sometimes answerable with "can I join the project you already have?"
  3. Nobody knows the menu. Not the courses (COMP 393? URES 295? INLS 651?) and not the fact that WXYC has shovel-ready projects with written specs waiting for someone to execute them. That's §1.
What the station will do for you. WXYC can hand you a scoped project with a written problem statement, supply the data and code review, sign as client where the course allows it, act as your mentor of record for URES 295, and write the end-of-term evaluation. What it cannot do is be your faculty sponsor for the routes that require faculty. Bring the prospectus to the station first; a named project with a client already attached is a far easier ask of a busy professor than "I'd like to do something with the radio station."

4Ground rules that apply to everything

One university policy — the Independent Study Courses Policy, formerly UPM #30 — governs every independent study, mentored research, internship, and honors thesis course at UNC:

  • A learning contract is mandatory. It specifies weekly hours, meeting cadence, assignments with due dates, and exactly how your grade is determined. Departments run it through the Online Learning Contract Manager (OLCM). Computer Science asks for approval at least 24 hours before the university deadline, so don't file at the buzzer.
  • Three hours of work per week per credit hour, minimum. A 3-credit independent study is a nine-hour-a-week commitment. This is a floor, not a suggestion, and your sponsor is expected to hold you to it. Scope accordingly.
  • There is always a final written product and a final assessment. A substantial paper, portfolio, or thesis. Pure code with no write-up does not satisfy the policy — budget for the artifact from week one. §10 shows how to make that artifact something the station actually keeps.
  • Registration closes early. The policy says the end of the first week of classes; the curricula office says the second week. The two official pages disagree, so treat "before the semester starts" as the real deadline and you are never wrong.
  • Caps. At most 12 graded independent-study credits count toward graduation (honors thesis hours excluded), and at most 12 in any one semester. Spend them deliberately.
  • Faculty caps. Two independent-study projects per faculty member per semester, plus two honors students — but CS explicitly notes a project may include multiple students.

5Routes where WXYC is your client or mentor

In these, the station has a formal, named role: it signs something, hosts you, or is the outside organization with the problem. The academic scaffolding already exists, so you are not inventing a syllabus. The cost is that the station has to be lined up before the term begins.

The classic client course: teams spend a semester building a working system the client "can use properly, and maintain and extend." The only credit vehicle where the station's tech lead can be your mentor of record, with no professor required. It is registered through the Office for Undergraduate Research with a learning contract, and the final product is a paper of five pages or more.

One credit is small enough that nobody has to be convinced of much, the sponsor problem disappears entirely, and it satisfies a gen-ed you have to satisfy anyway. Its sibling URES 195 is zero credits and free: it puts the research experience on your transcript without credit hours. Watch the spring deadline. Four weeks before spring classes puts the contract in early December, in the middle of fall finals. If you want URES for spring, the conversation with WXYC happens in October or November.

10Writing your one-page prospectus

Every route wants some version of the same document under a different name — prospectus, learning contract, project proposal. Write it once, well, and reuse it. One page is the right length. The template below is what a faculty sponsor needs in order to say yes without scheduling a meeting first, and it maps onto the fields OLCM will ask you for.

TITLE — one concrete noun phrase, not a theme. PROBLEM (3–4 sentences) What is broken, missing, or unknown today. Include one specific fact that proves you have actually looked — a number, a file, a behavior you observed. DELIVERABLE (2–3 sentences) What will exist at the end of the semester that does not exist now. Be physical: "a working X", "a report that answers Y", "a measurement of Z." Name the final written product explicitly — the policy requires one. METHOD / PLAN (5–8 bullets) Roughly one bullet per two weeks. Name the tools and the data you will use. Include the point where you show something to the station. HOW IT WILL BE EVALUATED What counts as success, in terms your sponsor can check without reading the code. Tie this to the final written product. TIME Hours per week × weeks, meeting the 3-hours-per-credit-hour floor. SUPERVISION Who at WXYC you work with, how often you meet, and what the station provides: data access, code review, the end-of-term evaluation. BACKGROUND Courses you have taken and anything you have built that is relevant. Two sentences.

Bring this to WXYC before you bring it to a professor. A prospectus that already names a station contact and a scoped project is a much easier ask than an open-ended request to supervise "something."

11Asking a faculty member

  • Start with someone who already knows you. An instructor from a course you did well in, with a project adjacent to what they teach, is a far better first ask than a stranger whose research area matches on paper.
  • Ask in person, or in a short and specific email. Attach the one-pager. Say what you want (to sponsor a 3-credit INLS 396 in spring), what it costs them (a meeting every two weeks and a grade at the end), and that the project and the client are already arranged.
  • Know the cap. Faculty are limited to two independent-study projects per semester — but a single project can hold more than one student. If a professor says they're full, ask whether you can join a project they are already running.
  • For URES 295, skip this step entirely. A WXYC mentor can sign as mentor of record. That is the whole reason URES is the recommended entry point.
  • If you get a no, ask for a redirect. "Is there someone in the department who'd be a better fit for this?" is a question most faculty answer generously.
  • Remember COMP 495's advisor rule. Your advisor may be UNC faculty from outside Computer Science, which widens the pool considerably for a project that spans music, archives, and code.

12Things this guide can't settle for you

These were researched and came back ambiguous. Verify each one with the relevant office before it becomes load-bearing in your plan.

  • COMP 393 grading basis. The catalog says letter-graded; the department's course table says pass/fail. Ask the CS undergraduate office — it determines whether the credits can carry a major GPA.
  • The add deadline. End of week 1 versus week 2 of classes, depending on which official page you read. Plan for before the term starts.
  • DATA 481 client submission. No documented process or portal for an internal UNC client to submit a project. Ask SDIS.
  • COMP 523 client application. The formal client-application channel is unverified after a staffing change. Email Dr. Stotts directly.
  • INLS 393 site supervisor. Whether a WXYC technical mentor clears the "practicing professional" bar is the course coordinator's judgment call, not a published rule.
  • INLS 396 instructor-approval form. Enrollment historically ran through a SILS form whose post-merger home is unresolved. Route through SDIS advising.
  • MUSC 395 non-major access. The catalog lists no restriction, but whether a non-major is actually admitted is unconfirmed.
  • MEJO seat availability. Whether non-Hussman students can get into MEJO 433 or 581 is unverified.
  • APPLES service-learning is probably not a path — the program partners with off-campus nonprofits, and WXYC is a campus student organization.
One structural change to know about. The School of Information and Library Science (SILS) no longer exists as a standalone school: it merged with the School of Data Science and Society into the School of Data and Information Sciences (SDIS) on July 1, 2026. All INLS course numbers and programs survive intact in the 2026–27 catalog, but old SILS forms pages currently return 404. If a link you find is dead, that is why — go to the SDIS site instead.
